CTI Types & Intelligence Lifecycle
What threat intelligence actually is and how it flows
Foundation

What is Cyber Threat Intelligence?

CTI is evidence-based knowledge about adversaries — who they are, what they want, how they operate, and what indicators they leave behind. The goal is to move from reactive security ("we got hacked") to proactive security ("we knew this attack was coming").

Raw data is not intelligence. A list of IP addresses is data. Understanding that those IPs belong to APT41's C2 infrastructure targeting Indian fintech companies is intelligence. The transformation requires context, analysis, and relevance to your organization.

Strategic
High-level trends for executives and boards. "APT groups are targeting Indian pharma sector this quarter." Non-technical. Used for budgets, risk decisions.
Operational
Specific campaigns and attack plans. "Phishing campaign targeting BFSI sector using fake RBI notices." Used by SOC managers, IR teams.
Tactical
TTPs — Tactics, Techniques, Procedures. How the attacker operates step-by-step. Used to tune detection rules and defenses.
Technical
Raw IOCs — IPs, domains, hashes, URLs. Machine-consumable, fed directly into SIEMs, firewalls, EDR. Short shelf life.

The Intelligence Lifecycle — 6 Phases

Every professional CTI team follows this cycle. It's iterative — the output of each cycle feeds requirements for the next.

1. Planning
Define Intelligence Requirements (IRs): What do we need to know? Who is likely to attack us? What assets are most critical?
2. Collection
Gather raw data from OSINT, dark web monitoring, vendor feeds, honeypots, internal telemetry, ISACs.
3. Processing
Normalize, filter, deduplicate raw data. Convert to usable formats (STIX, structured CSV, enriched JSON).
4. Analysis
Apply analytical frameworks (Diamond Model, Kill Chain). Assess attacker intent, capability, opportunity. Produce finished intelligence.
5. Dissemination
Deliver intelligence to the right audience in the right format — executive brief, SOC alert, SIEM rule, firewall block list.
6. Feedback
Consumers review intelligence quality. Was it actionable? Timely? Accurate? Drives improvement for next cycle.

The Pyramid of Pain
Why blocking hashes is useless and TTPs are gold
Must Know

David Bianco's Pyramid — The Most Important CTI Concept

The Pyramid of Pain (David Bianco, 2013) shows that different types of IOCs cause different levels of "pain" to attackers when you detect and block them. The higher up the pyramid, the harder it is for an attacker to change their behavior — and the more valuable that intelligence is.

Most organizations focus on the bottom. Elite CTI teams focus on the top.

TTPs
Tactics, Techniques & Procedures
Tough!
Tools
Malware, exploits, custom tooling
Challenging
Network/Host Artifacts
Registry keys, mutex, C2 URIs
Annoying
Domain Names
C2 domains, phishing domains
Simple
IP Addresses
C2 IPs, scanning IPs
Easy
Hash Values
MD5, SHA1, SHA256 of malware
Trivial
⚠️ The hard truth: Blocking a hash takes an attacker 30 seconds to bypass (recompile the malware). Blocking an IP takes minutes (new VPS). Detecting a TTP forces them to fundamentally change how they operate — that costs weeks of effort. Invest intelligence resources at the top of the pyramid.

03
Cyber Kill Chain
Lockheed Martin's 7-stage adversary model
Framework

The Kill Chain Concept

Originally a military targeting model, the Cyber Kill Chain (Lockheed Martin, 2011) maps the 7 stages an attacker must complete to achieve their objective. Defenders win by breaking the chain at any stage. The earlier you break it, the less damage occurs.

CTI analysts use the Kill Chain to map attack campaigns — understanding what stage attackers are at helps defenders prioritize response and predict next moves.

Stage 1
Reconnaissance
OSINT, scanning, LinkedIn, Shodan, DNS enumeration. Attacker maps the target.
Stage 2
Weaponization
Building malware, exploit + payload, phishing document. Happens offline — hardest to detect.
Stage 3
Delivery
Email phishing, watering hole, USB, supply chain. Malware reaches the target.
Stage 4
Exploitation
Vulnerability triggered, macro executed, user clicked. Code runs on target system.
Stage 5
Installation
Backdoor/RAT installed for persistent access. Registry Run keys, scheduled tasks, services.
Stage 6
C2
Malware phones home. Attacker gains interactive control. HTTPS, DNS tunneling, social media used as C2.
Stage 7
Actions on Objectives
Data exfil, ransomware, lateral movement, sabotage. The attacker's final goal executed.

Diamond Model of Intrusion Analysis
Structuring adversary analysis around 4 core features
Framework

The Diamond Model

Developed by Caltagirone, Pendergast & Betz (2013), the Diamond Model states that every intrusion event has four core features: Adversary, Capability, Infrastructure, and Victim. These four sit at the vertices of a diamond. Relationships between them reveal attacker behavior and allow attribution.

Where the Kill Chain is temporal (what happens when), the Diamond Model is structural (who uses what against whom).

Adversary
The threat actor — individual, group, nation-state. Includes intent (why), capability (what skills), and attribution. Often the most unknown vertex initially.
Capability
Tools, malware, exploits, TTPs used. What technical means does the adversary possess? Custom malware vs off-the-shelf tools tells you about resourcing.
Infrastructure
C2 servers, staging servers, phishing domains, bulletproof hosting, VPNs, compromised intermediaries. Connecting infrastructure reveals actor patterns.
Victim
The targeted organization, person, or asset. Understanding victim profile helps predict future targets in the same sector or geography.

Meta-Features (Extended Diamond)

Beyond the four vertices, analysts also track: Timestamp (when), Phase (which kill chain stage), Result (success/fail/unknown), Direction (infra to victim or reverse), and Methodology (social engineering, watering hole, etc.).

💎 Lab 4.1 — Apply Diamond Model to a real-world attack scenario Analysis ▶
💡Scenario: An Indian logistics company received a spear-phishing email with a malicious Excel attachment. The attachment executed a PowerShell downloader that connected to a domain registered 3 days prior. Use the Diamond Model to structure your analysis.
  1. 1
    Fill in each Diamond vertex from the scenario
    ADVERSARY: Known: Uses spear-phishing against logistics sector Unknown: Identity, nation-state vs criminal Hypothesis: Possibly TA505 or financially-motivated group (Excel + PS = common TA505 TTP) CAPABILITY: Delivery: Spear-phishing email with Excel .xlsm Exploit: Excel 4.0 macro (deprecated, bypasses many AVs) Malware: PowerShell downloader → unknown second-stage payload TTP: T1566.001 (Phishing Attachment), T1059.001 (PowerShell) INFRASTRUCTURE: C2 domain: tracklogistics-update[.]com (3 days old, Namecheap) C2 IP: 185.220.x.x (Bulletproof hosting, Eastern Europe) Email sender: Spoofed DHL domain VICTIM: Org type: Indian logistics company, 500 employees Targeted role: Finance team (email subject: "Invoice overdue") Asset at risk: Finance systems, banking credentials
  2. 2
    Use Infrastructure to pivot and find more victims
    The C2 IP can be searched on VirusTotal, Shodan, and PassiveDNS to find all domains resolving to it. If 10 other domains point to the same IP, all their victims are potentially related — you've now mapped a campaign, not just one incident.
  3. 3

    This adversary consistently uses Excel macros + PowerShell. Write a Wazuh/Splunk rule: alert when Excel spawns PowerShell. That single TTP-based rule will catch this attacker regardless of which domain or IP they use next time.

IOC Types & Enrichment Workflow
From raw indicator to actionable intelligence
Core Skill

Indicators of Compromise (IOCs)

An IOC is a forensic artifact that indicates a system may have been compromised. By itself, an IOC is just a data point. Enriched, it becomes intelligence — context about who used it, when, against whom, and what it does.

IOC enrichment means taking a raw value (like an IP address) and adding context from multiple sources: geolocation, ASN, reputation, associated malware families, linked domains, open ports, historical scan data.

STIX & TAXII — Structured Threat Sharing
The standard language and protocol for machine-readable CTI
Standard

Why We Need STIX/TAXII

Before STIX, every CTI vendor had a different format — PDFs, CSVs, proprietary JSON. You couldn't automate ingestion. STIX (Structured Threat Information Expression) is a standardized JSON schema for expressing threat intelligence. TAXII (Trusted Automated eXchange of Intelligence Information) is the transport protocol for sharing STIX over HTTPS.

Together: STIX = what you say, TAXII = how you send it. Like HTML and HTTP for the web.

STIX 2.1 Domain Objects (SDOs) — the building blocks

OSINT Recon Techniques
Actively gathering intelligence on threat actors and campaigns
Active Lab

Offensive OSINT for CTI Analysts

CTI analysts use the same OSINT tools as attackers — but to understand adversaries and map their infrastructure, not to attack targets. This is called threat actor infrastructure mapping. You start with one IOC and pivot until you've traced the entire campaign's infrastructure.

🕸️ Lab 8.1 — Map a phishing campaign infrastructure from a single domain OSINT ▶
⚠️Never visit suspicious URLs directly. Use URLScan, VirusTotal, or sandboxes. All investigation is passive — looking at data about domains/IPs, not connecting to them.
  1. 1
    Start with a phishing domain — WHOIS analysis
    whois phishingdomain-example.com # Record: Registrar, Registration date, Registrant email # Red flags: Registered today/this week, privacy-protected, Namecheap/GoDaddy
  2. 2
    DNS lookups — find the hosting IP
    dig phishingdomain-example.com +short # A record → hosting IP dig phishingdomain-example.com MX +short # Mail server (phishing infra) dig phishingdomain-example.com NS +short # Name server (actor reuses NS across domains) dig -x 185.220.101.47 +short # Reverse DNS of the IP
  3. 3
    Pivot: Find all domains on the same IP (passive DNS)
    # Option 1: VirusTotal passive DNS https://www.virustotal.com/gui/ip-address/HOSTING_IP/relations # Option 2: SecurityTrails (free tier) https://securitytrails.com/list/ip/HOSTING_IP # Option 3: RiskIQ Community (now Microsoft Defender TI, free tier) https://ti.defender.microsoft.com # Paste the IP → see all domains resolved to it historically
  4. 4
    Analyze domains for patterns (actor fingerprinting)
    Real actors reuse naming patterns. Look for: same TLD, similar keywords (update-, secure-, -login, -verify), registered same week, same registrar, same name server. Document the pattern in your CTI report.
    # Actor pattern identified from pivot: secure-hdfc-login.com # registered 2024-11-01 hdfc-verify-account.com # registered 2024-11-02 hdfcbank-update.net # registered 2024-11-03 # All on same IP, same Namecheap registrar, same NS = same actor campaign
  5. 5
    Check TLS certificate for linked infrastructure
    # Shodan SSL cert search — find all hosts using same cert https://www.shodan.io/search?query=ssl.cert.subject.cn:phishingdomain-example.com # Censys TLS cert pivot https://search.censys.io/certificates?q=parsed.names:phishingdomain-example.com # Actors reuse self-signed certs across all C2 nodes — this reveals entire infra

MISP — Malware Information Sharing Platform
The industry-standard open-source CTI platform
Platform Lab

What is MISP?

MISP (originally Malware Information Sharing Platform) is an open-source platform for collecting, storing, distributing, and sharing CTI in a structured way. It stores IOCs and threat data as Events, each linked to attributes (IOCs), galaxies (threat actors, MITRE ATT&CK), and tags.

Used by CERTs, ISACs, SOC teams, and MSSPs globally. MISP speaks STIX 2.1 natively and can share with other MISP instances via sync or TAXII.

Why Analyst Attribution Matters

Knowing who is attacking changes everything about your defensive posture. An APT28 intrusion means you're dealing with a sophisticated nation-state actor with deep resources — demand IR retainer, preserve forensics, notify CERT-In. A commodity ransomware infection (REvil affiliate) means patch management failure — different response entirely.

Attribution is probabilistic, not certain. Analysts say "we assess with medium confidence this is APT41" not "it definitely is." MITRE ATT&CK Navigator groups map actor TTPs — use them to tune your detections.

Dark Web Awareness for CTI
What analysts monitor, how, and what not to do
Awareness

The Dark Web in CTI Context

CTI analysts monitor the dark web not to participate in criminal activity, but to detect early warning signals — credentials for sale, data breach dumps, ransomware group announcements, zero-day offerings, and threat actor chatter about specific targets.

Key principle: Passive monitoring only. You observe, document, and report. You never register accounts, make purchases, or download files from criminal forums. Any such activity may constitute a criminal offense under IT Act Section 66 and IPC provisions.

Ransomware Leak Sites
Groups like LockBit, ALPHV, and Cl0p maintain .onion sites listing victims, exfiltrated data previews. Monitor for your client's name.
Credential Markets
Stealer logs (Redline, Vidar data) sold per record. Corporate VPN credentials, banking logins. Monitor for your domain on Have I Been Pwned, DeHashed.
Exploit Brokers
0-day exploits bought and sold. Nation-states and criminal groups purchase. Prices signal criticality — $2M+ for iOS zero-click.
Threat Actor Forums
XSS, Exploit.in, BreachForums (surface), RaidForums archives. Actors announce campaigns, recruit, sell access. Many are now monitored by law enforcement.
Initial Access Brokers
Attackers sell "RDP access to Fortune 500 company, $500". IABs specialize in initial access, sell to ransomware groups. Monitoring these predicts imminent attacks.
Paste Sites
Pastebin, Ghostbin, Hastebin used for data dumps, malware configs, stolen credentials. Monitor for company name, domain, email patterns.

Capstone

CTI Reports — What Makes Them Good

A good CTI report is actionable, audience-appropriate, and evidence-based. The most common mistake is writing for the wrong audience — a 20-page technical deep-dive sent to the CISO is useless. Know your consumer.

For a SOC/GRC job interview, having a polished CTI report in your GitHub portfolio demonstrates you can think like an analyst, not just run tools.

Executive Brief
1 page. Business impact, risk level, recommended action. No technical jargon. For CISO, CTO, board.
Tactical Report
3–5 pages. TTPs, IOCs, detection rules, analyst notes. For SOC manager, IR team.
Technical Deep Dive
Full malware analysis, YARA rules, network IOCs, complete evidence chain. For malware analysts.
