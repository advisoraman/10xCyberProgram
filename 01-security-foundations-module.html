What Is Cybersecurity?
Definition, scope, career paths, and why it matters for India
Foundation

Cybersecurity — A Working Definition

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting digital systems, networks, programs, and data from attack, damage, unauthorized access, and disruption. It is the intersection of technology, process, and people — no single tool or policy is sufficient on its own.

The field encompasses everything from writing secure code and configuring firewalls to drafting compliance policies and training employees not to click phishing links. Security is a property of the entire sociotechnical system, not of any individual component.

Bruce Schneier's formulation remains accurate: "Security is not a product, but a process." You don't achieve security once and move on — you manage it continuously as threats, technology, and organizations evolve.

"The goal of security is not to prevent all attacks — that is impossible. The goal is to make attacks sufficiently difficult, expensive, and detectable that adversaries choose easier targets or accept the risk of exposure."

— Security engineering principle

Why Cybersecurity Is Critical — The Indian Context

India's digital footprint is massive and growing: 900M+ internet users, 2nd largest smartphone market, UPI processing 10B+ transactions/month, Aadhaar used by 1.4B people, and a ₹8 lakh crore digital payments ecosystem. Each of these creates attack surface.

The threat is real and local: India was the 2nd most attacked country in Asia-Pacific in 2023 (IBM X-Force). CERT-In received over 1.4 million cybersecurity incident reports in 2022. Indian healthcare, BFSI, and government are primary targets. The AIIMS ransomware attack (2022) disrupted patient care for weeks. The CoWIN data leak exposed vaccination records of crores of citizens.

The workforce gap: India needs 1+ million cybersecurity professionals; only ~140,000 are currently working in the field (NASSCOM 2023). Entry-level SOC analysts with verifiable skills command ₹5–12 LPA. GRC specialists at Big 4 earn ₹8–18 LPA with 2 years experience.

SOC Analyst
Monitor alerts, triage incidents, escalate threats. Entry point: L1 at MSSP. Requires: log analysis, SIEM, incident response basics. 0–2 yrs experience.
GRC Analyst
Governance, Risk, Compliance. ISO 27001, DPDP Act, risk registers. Big 4 and BFSI focused. Requires policy writing, audit skills, framework knowledge.
Penetration Tester
Simulate attacks against authorized targets. Report vulnerabilities. Requires: CEH/OSCP, hands-on hacking skills, report writing. 1–3 yrs lab experience.
Cloud Security Eng.
Secure AWS/Azure/GCP environments. IAM, CSPM, cloud-native security tools. Fastest growing role. Requires cloud platform + security knowledge.
Threat Intelligence
Track threat actors, IOCs, campaigns. Feed intel to SOC and IR teams. Requires OSINT skills, CTI platforms (MISP), analytical writing.
CISO / Security Mgr
Oversees security programme, budgets, board reporting. Requires 10+ years across domains. Usually a generalist with deep expertise in 2–3 areas.

The CIA Triad — Deep Dive
Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — the foundational security model
Core Model

The CIA Triad — Why It Anchors Everything

Every security control, every policy, every attack can be mapped to one or more of the three CIA properties. When you analyse a vulnerability, you ask: "Does this compromise confidentiality, integrity, availability, or all three?" When you design a control, you ask: "Which CIA property does this protect?" It's the lingua franca of information security.

The model is deceptively simple. The hard part is that the three properties often trade off against each other: maximum confidentiality (encrypt everything, share nothing) reduces availability; maximum availability (always accessible, minimal auth) reduces confidentiality. Security engineering is about finding the right balance for your risk context.

C
Confidentiality
Information is accessible only to those authorised to access it. Prevents unauthorized disclosure. Not just about encryption — also about access control, data classification, and need-to-know.
Examples: Encrypting customer PII at rest and in transit. Role-based access so HR sees salary data but Finance does not see medical records. VPN for remote worker traffic.
Attacks: Data breach, eavesdropping, MITM, credential theft, SQL injection dumping database
I
Integrity
Information is accurate, complete, and has not been modified in an unauthorised way. Includes both data integrity (the stored data) and system integrity (the software/config). Non-repudiation is a related concept.
Examples: SHA-256 hashes to verify downloaded files. Digital signatures on software. Database audit logs that record every change with who made it and when. File Integrity Monitoring (Wazuh FIM).
Attacks: Malware modifying system files, SQL injection altering records, ransomware encrypting files, DNS poisoning, supply chain tampering
A
Availability
Systems and information are accessible when needed by authorised users. Includes uptime, performance, and resilience. Often the CIA property that directly costs businesses money when violated — downtime = lost revenue.
Examples: Load balancers, redundant data centres, backup power (UPS), DDoS mitigation, cloud auto-scaling. RTO/RPO targets in BCP/DRP. Health monitoring and automated failover.
Attacks: DDoS, ransomware, disk failure without backup, network outage, hardware destruction, Slowloris exhausting web server connections

Real-World CIA Triad Analysis — AIIMS Ransomware Attack (2022)

In November 2022, the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS) Delhi suffered a ransomware attack. Map it to CIA:

Confidentiality violated: Patient records — including prominent politicians' medical data — were potentially exfiltrated. The attackers reportedly demanded ₹200 crore (later reported as a figure in crypto).

Integrity violated: Ransomware encrypted files, rendering them unreadable and effectively corrupted from the hospital's perspective. Systems could not be trusted even after decryption (potential backdoors).

Availability severely violated: Servers offline for 15+ days. Lab reports, billing, patient admission systems all went manual. Patient care was disrupted. This is the most visible impact.

Root causes (reported): unpatched systems, no multi-factor authentication, inadequate network segmentation, insufficient backups. Every one of these is a foundational security failure.

Extended Models — Beyond CIA

The CIA triad is sometimes extended with additional properties: Authenticity — verifying that communication or data is genuinely from the claimed source (digital signatures). Non-repudiation — the sender cannot later deny having sent the message (audit logs, digital signatures). Accountability — actions can be traced to individual users (user-specific credentials, audit trails, no shared accounts). These are often grouped as the Parkerian Hexad or the CIA+ model.

III
The AAA Model
Authentication, Authorization, Accounting — the identity security framework
Identity

AAA — How Systems Control and Track Access

Every time you access a system, three questions are answered: Are you who you claim to be? (Authentication) Are you allowed to do this? (Authorization) What did you do, and when? (Accounting/Auditing). Together, these three form the AAA framework — the foundation of access control in every enterprise system.

AAA is implemented in networks via RADIUS, TACACS+, and 802.1X. In cloud via IAM policies. In applications via OAuth 2.0 and OIDC. Understanding AAA is prerequisite to understanding firewalls, VPNs, cloud security, and identity management — all of which implement these three concepts.

Authentication
Verifying identity — proving you are who you claim to be. Not what you're allowed to do — just confirming your identity.
Something you know: Password, PIN, security question
Something you have: OTP token, smart card, FIDO2 key
Something you are: Fingerprint, face, retina (biometrics)

MFA combines 2+ factors — significantly stronger than single-factor. Required by DPDP Act for data fiduciaries.
Authorization
Determining permissions — what an authenticated identity is allowed to do. Runs after authentication, not before.
DAC — Discretionary Access Control: owner grants access
MAC — Mandatory Access Control: labels (Secret, Top Secret)
RBAC — Role-Based: permissions tied to roles (Admin, User)
ABAC — Attribute-Based: context-aware (location, device, time)

Principle: grant minimum permissions needed. Regularly review and revoke.
Accounting / Auditing
Recording what happened — who did what, when, from where, to which resource, with what outcome.
Audit logs: Windows Event Logs (4624, 4625, 4688...)
Syslog: Linux auth.log, application logs
SIEM: Aggregated, searchable, alert-capable
CERT-In requires 180-day log retention

Without accounting: no forensics, no incident investigation, no compliance evidence.

IV
The Threat Landscape
Vulnerabilities, threats, risks, and the risk management equation
Threats

Vulnerability, Threat, Risk — Three Different Things

These three terms are often conflated but mean very different things. A vulnerability is a weakness in a system — an unpatched CVE, a misconfigured firewall rule, an employee who hasn't had security training. A vulnerability alone causes no harm.

A threat is any potential event that could exploit a vulnerability — a ransomware group, a disgruntled employee, a software bug. A threat without a vulnerability has nothing to exploit.

Risk is the combination of likelihood (how probable is a threat exploiting a vulnerability?) and impact (how bad would it be?). Risk = Likelihood × Impact. This equation drives every resource allocation decision in security. You don't treat every risk equally — you treat the highest risks first.

The Risk Equation in Practice

An ancient server running Telnet (vulnerability: cleartext protocol, unpatched OS) in a sealed, disconnected research lab with no internet access has a low risk — the threat (external attacker) has no pathway to reach it. The same server on your public-facing DMZ has a critical risk. Same vulnerability, radically different risk, because the threat landscape changed.

Risk can be reduced by: eliminating the vulnerability (patch, reconfigure), reducing likelihood (firewalls, IDS, monitoring), reducing impact (backups, incident response plan, cyber insurance), or accepting the residual risk after controls are applied.

V
Threat Actors & Motives
Who attacks, why they attack, and what resources they have
Adversary

Knowing Your Adversary Changes Your Defence

Not all attackers are the same. A nation-state group (APT) with unlimited budget and zero-day exploits requires different defences than an opportunistic script kiddie running automated vulnerability scanners. Understanding who is likely to target you — based on your industry, size, geography, and data — helps you prioritize defences correctly.

Threat actor profiling is a core CTI (Cyber Threat Intelligence) skill. The key attributes: motivation (why are they attacking?), capability (what tools and skills do they have?), opportunity (what access or vulnerability enables the attack?), and target selection (who do they attack?).

🏛️ Nation-State / APT
Government-sponsored groups (APT28/Russia, APT41/China, Lazarus/North Korea). Motivation: espionage, sabotage, financial gain (sanctions evasion). Capability: highest — zero-days, custom malware, unlimited budget. Target: government, defence, critical infrastructure, BFSI. India-specific risk: HIGH from Chinese and Pakistani state actors.
CRITICAL
💰 Cybercriminals
Financially motivated organised crime groups. Ransomware-as-a-Service operators, card fraud rings, BEC (Business Email Compromise) groups. Motivation: money. Capability: moderate to high — use affiliate model to scale. Target: anyone who can pay or has valuable data. Indian BFSI and healthcare are primary targets.
HIGH
😤 Insider Threat
Employees, contractors, partners with legitimate access. Disgruntled employees (intentional harm), negligent users (accidental), or compromised accounts (used by external attackers). Motivation: revenge, financial (selling data), ideology, or simply carelessness. Most damaging per incident — have authorised access and know the systems.
HIGH
✊ Hacktivists
Ideologically motivated attackers. Anonymous, Team Poison. Motivation: political, social, environmental causes. Capability: low to moderate — usually DDoS and website defacement. Target: organizations whose actions they oppose. Common during political events, elections, social controversies in India.
MEDIUM
🧑‍💻 Script Kiddies
Inexperienced attackers using pre-built tools without understanding them. Motivation: notoriety, curiosity, vandalism. Capability: low — running automated scanners, using public exploits. Opportunistic — attack whatever is vulnerable, not targeting specifically. Volume-based: they scan everything, and unpatched systems get hit regardless of importance.
LOW–MED
🔍 Competitive Intelligence
Corporate espionage — stealing trade secrets, customer lists, pricing, R&D. May use hired hackers, social engineering, or corrupt insiders. Motivation: competitive advantage. Target: tech companies, pharmaceutical R&D (significant for Indian pharma), startups before IPO. Often goes unreported.
MEDIUM
VI
Attack Categories
Active vs passive, internal vs external, targeted vs opportunistic
Classification

Why Classification Matters

Attack classification isn't academic — it directly affects detection and response strategies. A passive attack (eavesdropping) leaves no trace on the target system; your firewall can't see it. An active attack modifies something and can be detected. Understanding these distinctions shapes how you design your monitoring and response capabilities.

👁️ Passive Attacks
▸
Eavesdropping / Sniffing: Monitoring network traffic to capture data, credentials, or metadata. Leaves no trace on the target. Example: capturing cleartext FTP credentials on a shared network.
▸
Traffic Analysis: Even encrypted traffic reveals patterns — who communicates with whom, how often, data volumes. Used for profiling and timing attacks.
▸
Reconnaissance: OSINT gathering before an attack — LinkedIn scraping, Shodan scanning, DNS enumeration. Entirely passive from the target's perspective.
Detection challenge: Passive attacks are hard to detect because nothing is changed on the target. Prevention focuses on encryption (TLS, VPN, SSH) so captured traffic is useless.
⚡ Active Attacks
▸
Masquerade: Impersonating a legitimate user or system to gain access. Involves forged credentials, spoofed IP addresses, or fake identity.
▸
Replay Attack: Capturing a valid authentication sequence and replaying it later to gain access — without knowing the actual credentials.
▸
Modification: Altering data in transit or at rest. SQL injection modifying database records. File system modifications by malware. DNS cache poisoning.
▸
Denial of Service: Preventing legitimate users from accessing resources. DDoS, Slowloris, resource exhaustion, ransomware encrypting files.
Detection opportunity: Active attacks modify system state — network traffic anomalies, log entries, file changes — which IDS/SIEM can detect.
Targeted Attack
Specific victim selected deliberately. APT groups research target, craft spear-phishing. Time-consuming, expensive. You've been chosen because of what you have or know. Harder to prevent with generic controls.
Opportunistic Attack
Mass scanning for easy victims. Script kiddies, automated exploit kits. "Spray and pray" — hit everything that's vulnerable. Basic patching and configuration significantly reduces exposure.
Internal Attack
Originates from inside the perimeter. Has network access already. Can bypass perimeter controls. Requires internal network segmentation, least privilege, and behavioural monitoring to detect.
External Attack
Originates from outside the network. Must penetrate perimeter defences first. Firewall and IPS most effective here. Once inside, behaves like internal attack.

Common Attack Types
Phishing, social engineering, MitM, injection, brute force, and more
Attack Types

The Attack Surface Is Humans + Technology

The majority of successful attacks begin with the human layer before moving to technical exploitation. A technically perfect firewall cannot prevent an employee from being manipulated into emailing credentials to an attacker. Understanding attack types bridges the technical and human dimensions of security.

Malware — Types, Behaviour & Detection
Virus, worm, trojan, ransomware, rootkit, spyware, keylogger — complete taxonomy
Malware

Malware — Malicious Software in All Forms

Malware (malicious software) is any software intentionally designed to cause harm to a computer, server, client, or network. Understanding malware types is foundational — they differ in how they spread, what they do, how they persist, and how they're detected. Each type demands different defensive and response measures.

IX
Security Controls
Preventive, detective, corrective, and deterrent — the control taxonomy
Controls

Controls — The Implementation of Security Policy

A security control is any safeguard, countermeasure, or action taken to reduce security risk. Controls are the bridge between policy ("we must protect customer data") and reality ("here's the AES-256 encryption, access control list, and audit log that implements that policy").

Controls are classified in two ways: by function (what they do) and by type (how they do it). Understanding both dimensions helps you identify gaps: if you have many preventive controls but no detective controls, you may be breached without knowing it.

Preventive
Stop an attack before it succeeds. Firewall blocking inbound connections. MFA preventing unauthorized login. Encryption making stolen data useless. Input validation preventing SQL injection. Goal: make the attack impossible or fail.
Detective
Identify that an attack has occurred or is occurring. IDS/SIEM alerts. CCTV. Audit logs showing failed logins. File integrity monitoring alerting to modified system files. Goal: detect breaches, minimize dwell time.
Corrective
Restore normal operations after a security event. Restoring from backup after ransomware. Patching the vulnerability that was exploited. Revoking compromised credentials. IR plan execution. Goal: reduce impact, restore service.
Deterrent
Discourage potential attackers without blocking them. CCTV signage. "All activity is monitored and logged" banners. Legal warnings. Visible security presence. Goal: make attacker choose an easier target.
Compensating
Alternative controls when primary controls can't be implemented. Can't patch a legacy medical device OS (manufacturer doesn't support it)? Network isolate it instead. Goal: achieve equivalent security outcome differently.
Recovery
Restore capabilities after a major incident. Disaster recovery plan. Backup restoration procedures. Business continuity arrangements. IR retainer. Goal: ensure organization survives even severe incidents.

Control Types — Administrative, Technical, Physical

Administrative (Management) controls: Policies, procedures, training, standards. "No sharing of passwords" policy. Annual security awareness training. Background checks for new employees. Vendor security assessment questionnaires. These define expected behaviour but rely on people following them.

Technical (Logical) controls: Software and hardware enforcing security. Firewalls, IDS/IPS, encryption, MFA, access control lists, audit logs, antivirus, SIEM. These enforce requirements automatically regardless of human behaviour. Generally more reliable than administrative controls.

Physical controls: Protect physical access to systems and facilities. Biometric locks, CCTV cameras, security guards, server room access logs, cable locks on laptops, badge readers, visitor logs. Prevent physical theft, hardware tampering, and unauthorized physical access.

Defence-in-Depth
Layered security — why no single control is sufficient
Strategy

The Onion Model of Security

Defence-in-depth (also called layered security or the onion model) means implementing multiple independent layers of security controls so that if one fails, others still protect the asset. It is the most important strategic principle in security architecture.

Single points of failure are the enemy of security. If your only defence is a perimeter firewall and an attacker bypasses it (via phishing, VPN credentials, insider threat), they have unrestricted access to everything. With defence-in-depth, each layer forces the attacker to defeat additional controls — increasing cost, time, noise, and chance of detection.

The concept comes from military doctrine: multiple lines of defence (outposts, fortifications, fallback positions) mean an enemy who breaks through the first line still faces resistance. In security: the perimeter is not the only line.

Policies, Procedures & Awareness
Outermost layer — the human and process foundation. Security training, acceptable use policy, incident response plan, background checks.
Physical Security
Locked server rooms, badge access, CCTV, cable locks. Prevents physical theft and tampering.
Perimeter Security
Firewall, IDS/IPS, DMZ, DDoS mitigation. First technical line — filters what enters the network.
Network Security
VLANs, network segmentation, ACLs, VPN. Limits lateral movement if perimeter is breached.
Host Security
EDR, antivirus, host firewall, OS hardening, patch management. Protects individual systems.
Application Security
WAF, secure coding, SSDLC, input validation. Protects applications from exploitation.
Data Security
Encryption at rest and in transit, access controls, DLP, data classification. Final line — protects the asset itself.
💡 Key insight: An attacker who bypasses the perimeter firewall still faces network segmentation (VLAN), then host security (EDR catches the malware), then application security (WAF blocks injection), then data security (encrypted data is useless). Each layer forces additional effort and creates detection opportunities.

Least Privilege & Need-to-Know
Core access control principles that reduce attack surface dramatically
Principles

Least Privilege — The Single Most Effective Security Principle

The principle of least privilege (PoLP) states that every user, process, or system component should have access to only the minimum resources and permissions necessary to perform its function — nothing more. This is the most consistently effective security principle across all domains.

Why it works: It reduces the blast radius of any security incident. If an attacker compromises a low-privilege account, they can only do what that account can do. If they compromise an admin account, they can do everything. Keeping admin privileges rare and time-limited is one of the highest-value security investments an organization can make.

User Accounts
Regular users get no admin rights. Admin tasks require a separate, dedicated admin account with MFA. Service accounts get only the permissions the specific service needs — not domain admin. Audit quarterly.
Database Access
Web application's DB user should have SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE on specific tables — never DROP, CREATE, or system privileges. Separate read-only accounts for reporting tools. Never connect as root/sa.
File System
Files and directories have only the permissions users need. /etc/shadow readable only by root. Web server reads web root but cannot write to system directories. Regular audits of overpermissive directories.
Cloud IAM
AWS/Azure roles and policies grant specific actions on specific resources. Avoid AdministratorAccess unless necessary. Use IAM Access Analyzer to find overpermissive policies. Rotate/expire access keys.
Application Processes
Web server runs as www-data, not root. Docker containers run as non-root user. Linux capabilities instead of full sudo. If the web server is compromised, attacker is limited to www-data permissions.
Network Access
Firewall rules: allow only required ports between specific hosts. Developers cannot access production databases directly — go through application layer. DBAs access DB via bastion host, not direct internet.

Need-to-Know — Confidentiality-Focused Least Privilege

Need-to-know is the confidentiality-specific version of least privilege: users should only have access to the information they need to perform their specific job function. An HR manager needs employee salary data — they don't need access to the source code repository. A developer needs production logs for debugging — they don't need to see customer PII in those logs.

Need-to-know is implemented through data classification (categorize data by sensitivity), access controls (enforce at system level), and data masking (show developers logs with PII masked out). Violating need-to-know is a common DPDP Act compliance gap — organizations give too broad data access by default.

🔒 Lab 11.1 — Implement least privilege on Linux: file permissions, sudo, and service accounts Linux Lab ▶
  1. 1
    Audit and fix file permissions
    # Find world-writable files (any user can write — dangerous) find /etc -type f -perm -o+w 2>/dev/null find /var/www -type f -perm -o+w 2>/dev/null # Find SUID/SGID binaries (run as owner, often root) find / -type f \( -perm -4000 -o -perm -2000 \) 2>/dev/null # Fix an overpermissive file ls -la /var/www/html/config.php # If it shows -rw-rw-rw- (world readable — bad!) sudo chmod 640 /var/www/html/config.php sudo chown www-data:www-data /var/www/html/config.php # Now: -rw-r----- → www-data can read/write, group can read, others nothing
  2. 2
    Create a restricted service account
    # Create a service account with no shell (cannot login interactively) sudo useradd -r -s /usr/sbin/nologin -d /var/lib/myservice myservice-user # -r: system account | -s: shell = nologin | -d: home directory # Run a service as this restricted user instead of root # In /etc/systemd/system/myservice.service: # User=myservice-user # Group=myservice-user # If this service is compromised, attacker is limited to myservice-user # Cannot ssh in, cannot access other users' files, cannot escalate easily
  3. 3
    Configure sudo with minimal required permissions
    # Bad practice (too much privilege): # developer ALL=(ALL) NOPASSWD: ALL # Good practice (only what's needed): sudo visudo # Add: developer ALL=(ALL) /usr/bin/systemctl restart nginx # Developer can ONLY restart nginx — nothing else # Check what a user can sudo sudo -l -U developer # Shows exactly what commands that user can run with sudo # Audit: find users with ALL privileges grep -E "ALL.*ALL" /etc/sudoers /etc/sudoers.d/*
XII
Zero Trust Principles
Never trust, always verify — the modern security paradigm
Modern Paradigm

From Castle-and-Moat to Zero Trust

The traditional security model was "castle and moat": a hard perimeter (firewall) protects a trusted interior. Once inside the network, devices and users were implicitly trusted. This model assumed the perimeter held and that insider threats were rare. Both assumptions are now demonstrably false.

Zero Trust assumes: the network is always hostile, external threats exist, and internal threats exist. No user, device, or network location is trusted by default — trust must be continually earned through authentication, authorization, device health checks, and behavioural monitoring. This is encapsulated in the phrase "never trust, always verify."

Verify Explicitly
Always authenticate and authorize based on all available data points: identity, location, device health, service/workload, data classification, and anomalies. Not just username + password.
Least Privilege Access
Limit access to just-in-time, just-enough-access. Time-bound elevated privileges. Risk-based adaptive policies — higher sensitivity data requires stronger auth. Rotate credentials regularly.
Assume Breach
Design as if attackers are already inside. Minimize blast radius via segmentation. End-to-end encryption. Use analytics to detect threats, gain visibility, and improve defences.
Identity Is the Perimeter
The network perimeter is dissolved. Cloud, mobile, remote work mean users and data are everywhere. Identity — verified and continuously validated — becomes the new security boundary.
Microsegmentation
Divide the network into small, isolated zones. Each workload in its own segment with firewall rules. East-west traffic controlled as strictly as north-south. Lateral movement requires defeating per-segment controls.
Continuous Monitoring
Verify not just at login but throughout the session. Device compliance checked continuously. User behaviour baselines — deviation triggers step-up authentication or session termination.
📌 India enterprise context: Banks like HDFC and ICICI, IT majors like TCS and Infosys, and all BPOs handling sensitive customer data are actively implementing Zero Trust frameworks. CERT-In's 2022 directions implicitly require Zero Trust principles — 6-hour breach reporting assumes you have monitoring everywhere, not just at the perimeter. DPDP Act compliance requires continuous data access controls, not perimeter-only security.

Windows Security Fundamentals
Users, groups, permissions, registry, Active Directory, and Event Viewer
Windows

Why Windows Security Is Foundational

Over 70% of enterprise endpoints and servers run Windows. Active Directory is the identity backbone of most corporate networks worldwide — including virtually every Indian enterprise with more than 50 employees. A SOC analyst who cannot navigate Windows security events, understand AD, or work with Group Policy will miss the majority of real-world incidents.

Windows security has evolved significantly — Windows 10/11 and Server 2019/2022 have strong security features. Most Windows breaches are due to misconfiguration or unpatched vulnerabilities, not fundamental Windows design flaws.

🪟 Lab 13.1 — Windows security hardening and Event Viewer analysis Windows Lab ▶
  1. 1
    Audit local users and groups
    # List all local users Get-LocalUser | Select-Object Name, Enabled, LastLogon, PasswordLastSet # List all local groups Get-LocalGroup # See who is in Administrators group (CRITICAL) Get-LocalGroupMember -Group "Administrators" # Every user here has full system control — minimize this list # Check for users with no password expiry (security risk) Get-LocalUser | Where-Object {$_.PasswordNeverExpires}
  2. 2
    Check and harden the local security policy
    # Check password policy net accounts # Look for: Minimum password length, Maximum password age, lockout threshold # Harden via Local Security Policy (secpol.msc) or Group Policy # Recommended minimums: # Minimum password length: 12 characters # Password complexity: Enabled # Maximum password age: 90 days # Account lockout: 5 failed attempts → 30 min lockout # Audit: log success AND failure for logon events
  3. 3
    Analyse security events in Event Viewer
    # Find all failed login attempts in last 24 hours (Event ID 4625) Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{ LogName='Security'; Id=4625 StartTime=(Get-Date).AddDays(-1) } | Select-Object TimeCreated, @{N='User';E={$_.Properties[5].Value}}, @{N='Source';E={$_.Properties[19].Value}} | Format-Table # Find successful logins (Event ID 4624) Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='Security';Id=4624} \ -MaxEvents 20 # Find new user accounts created (Event ID 4720) Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{LogName='Security';Id=4720} # Any unexpected entries here = potential compromise (backdoor account)
  4. 4
    Check startup locations for persistence
    # Check registry Run keys (common malware persistence location) Get-ItemProperty "HKLM:\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run" Get-ItemProperty "HKCU:\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run" # Anything unexpected here = potential malware persistence # Check scheduled tasks Get-ScheduledTask | Where-Object {$_.State -eq "Ready"} | \ Select-Object TaskName, TaskPath, @{N="Author";E={$_.Principal.UserId}} # Check Windows services for suspicious entries Get-Service | Where-Object {$_.StartType -eq "Automatic"} | \ Sort-Object Name | Select-Object Name, Status, DisplayName

🐧 Lab 14.1 — Linux security hardening checklist Linux Lab ▶
  1. 1
    Users, groups, and privilege audit
    # List all users with login shells (can login interactively) grep -v "nologin\|false" /etc/passwd | cut -d: -f1 # Find users with UID=0 (root-equivalent — should be ONLY root) awk -F: '($3 == 0) {print}' /etc/passwd # Find users with empty passwords (CRITICAL security issue) sudo awk -F: '($2 == "") {print $1}' /etc/shadow # List all groups and members cat /etc/group | grep -v "^#" # Check sudoers for dangerous ALL=(ALL) NOPASSWD: ALL entries sudo cat /etc/sudoers | grep -v "^#" | grep -v "^$"
  2. 2
    Running processes and network connections
    # List all listening ports and which process owns them ss -tulpn # -t: TCP | -u: UDP | -l: listening | -p: show process | -n: numeric # Review every listening port — is this service intentional? # List all established connections (active network connections) ss -tnp state established # Find processes running as root ps aux | awk '$1 == "root" {print}' # Minimize root processes — each is an escalation target if compromised # Find processes with network connections but no associated file # (possible deleted-but-running malware) ls -la /proc/*/exe 2>/dev/null | grep deleted
  3. 3
    Service hardening — disable unnecessary services
    # List all enabled services systemctl list-unit-files --type=service --state=enabled # Disable services you don't need (principle of least service) sudo systemctl disable --now telnet # cleartext — always disable sudo systemctl disable --now rsh # cleartext remote shell — disable sudo systemctl disable --now finger # user info leakage — disable # Check if unnecessary packages are installed dpkg -l | grep telnet sudo apt remove --purge telnetd # remove if found
  4. 4
    Key log files every analyst must know
    # Authentication events (logins, sudo, su) sudo tail -50 /var/log/auth.log # System events (kernel, daemon, cron, general) sudo tail -50 /var/log/syslog # Login history (who logged in and from where) last # successful logins lastb # failed login attempts (sudo needed) w # currently logged in users who # who is logged in # Kernel messages (hardware errors, module loads) dmesg | tail -30 # All log files in /var/log ls -la /var/log/
  5. 5
    Kernel and system hardening with sysctl
    # Disable IP forwarding (unless this is a router/firewall) sudo sysctl -w net.ipv4.ip_forward=0 # Enable SYN cookies (DDoS protection) sudo sysctl -w net.ipv4.tcp_syncookies=1 # Disable ICMP redirects (prevent routing manipulation) sudo sysctl -w net.ipv4.conf.all.accept_redirects=0 sudo sysctl -w net.ipv4.conf.all.send_redirects=0 # Ignore ICMP broadcast (Smurf DDoS protection) sudo sysctl -w net.ipv4.icmp_echo_ignore_broadcasts=1 # Make permanent: add to /etc/sysctl.conf sudo sysctl -p # reload after editing /etc/sysctl.conf

Capstone

Interview Questions on Foundations — Every Role Asks These

Whether you're applying for SOC analyst, GRC analyst, or penetration tester, the interviewer will start with foundations to gauge your understanding. These questions seem simple but reveal whether you've truly internalized the concepts or just memorized definitions.

🎯 Interview Preparation — High-frequency foundations questions Interview Prep ▶
  1. 1
    Explain the CIA Triad with a banking example
    Sample answer: "The CIA Triad stands for Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability. In a banking context: Confidentiality means only you and the bank can see your account balance and transaction history — not other customers or unauthorized staff. We achieve this through encryption of data at rest and in transit, and role-based access control so only authorized employees can view accounts. Integrity means your balance accurately reflects all transactions — no unauthorized modifications. We achieve this through audit logs, database transaction integrity, and digital signatures on financial messages. Availability means you can access your account and make transactions when you need to. We achieve this through redundant servers, DDoS protection, and disaster recovery plans. A ransomware attack on a bank would violate all three: encrypted files compromise availability, exfiltrated records violate confidentiality, and corrupted data violates integrity."
  2. 2
    What is the difference between a vulnerability, a threat, and a risk?
    Vulnerability: A weakness or flaw that can be exploited. Example: An unpatched Apache server with a known CVE. Threat: An agent or event that could exploit the vulnerability. Example: A ransomware group scanning for vulnerable Apache servers. Risk: The combination of likelihood and impact. Risk = the chance that the ransomware group exploits our Apache server × the business impact of that happening. Key insight: "We can't fix all vulnerabilities, so we prioritize based on risk — which vulnerabilities are most likely to be exploited, and what would the impact be? That's what risk management decides."
  3. 3
    What is defence-in-depth and why is it important?
    Defence-in-depth means implementing multiple independent layers of security controls so that if one fails, others still protect the asset. Why important: No single control is perfect. A firewall can be bypassed via phishing (human layer). If the perimeter is the ONLY control, bypass = game over. With defence-in-depth: - Phishing bypasses the firewall (perimeter layer fails) - But EDR detects the malware execution (host layer catches it) - Or SIEM alerts on unusual behaviour (detection layer) - Or network segmentation prevents lateral movement (network layer) Each layer forces the attacker to defeat additional controls — increasing their cost, time, and chance of detection. Real example: The AIIMS breach succeeded partly because there was no defence-in-depth — when the endpoint was compromised, there was little to stop lateral movement to servers.
  4. 4
    What is the principle of least privilege? Give an example.
    Principle of Least Privilege: Every user, process, or system should have only the minimum permissions necessary for their role — nothing more. Example 1 — User accounts: A content writer in a media company needs to log into WordPress and write articles. They should NOT have admin access to the WordPress dashboard, the underlying database, or the web server. If their account is phished, the damage is limited to what they could do. Example 2 — Database: A web application that reads products from a database should connect as a user with SELECT permission only on the products table — not as the database admin. If there's a SQL injection, the attacker can read products but cannot DROP tables or read other schemas. Why it matters: Every excess privilege is a potential escalation path for an attacker who compromises that account.
